The problem

Most content programs have no memory.

Teams publish every week and learn almost nothing every week. It isn't that teams don't care about data; the loop that should carry it is broken in three places:

The stages don't talk

Ideate, write, send, and measure live in separate tools, owned by separate people. The brief doesn't know the data; the CRM doesn't know the personas. Every handoff drops information.

So results get reported, then forgotten

Metrics land in a dashboard and a Monday meeting, and that's where they die. By the time next week's topic is chosen, last week's lesson is gone.

So every week starts from zero

No memory means no compounding. Fifty-two weeks of publishing produces fifty-two disconnected guesses, not one system that gets smarter.

The real cost isn't the wasted effort. It's the compounding that never happens. The hypothesis behind SignalLoop: wire the stages into one system and give the loop a memory, and engagement stops being a report. It becomes the signal that writes the next round.

The thesis

The judgment drives the automation, not the other way around

The hard part of marketing isn't sending email; it's knowing who to say what to. So the one thing the AI never decides is the segmentation. I define each audience and its angle; the engine executes that judgment at scale. To prove it isn't hard-coded to one case, the same engine runs two very different companies, and it segments them differently, because the right logic depends on the market:

What stays human

  • How each market segments
  • Positioning and the angle per audience
  • Business context and the campaign objective
  • What ships and what gets rejected

What the system runs

  • Drafting and persona variation
  • CRM execution and logging
  • Per-segment measurement
  • Synthesis and next-round recommendations

This is not an AI that does marketing. It is marketing judgment, executed by a machine.

How it works

Every send teaches the next one.

You give it one topic. It writes a version for each audience, sends them through the CRM, and tracks what lands. What it learns decides what gets written next.

The proof

Same engine, different market judgment

Anyone can claim their system is strategic. The test is whether the strategy changes when the market does. So the same engine runs two very different companies, and the segmentation logic is different for each, on purpose:

Ramp: finance automation

Segments by organizational role

Spend touches finance leadership, accounting, and every employee who buys.

  • Finance Leader (economic buyer)
  • Controller (operator)
  • Employee Spender (end user)

Square: SMB commerce

Segments by business stage

One owner wears every hat, so maturity matters more than title.

  • New Seller
  • Growing Business
  • Multi-Location Operator

Swap one company-profile file and the pipeline changes who it targets, what it says, how it segments, and which audience wins. Nothing else in the code changes.

Run the loop yourself

Pick a company and run one full loop: generate the content, distribute it through the CRM, measure engagement, and decide what to publish next. Every run is saved, so the recommendations shift as results pile up.

Content comes from each company's profile. CRM calls are built exactly as they would be sent to HubSpot, in mock mode until a CRM account is connected. Engagement is simulated and no emails are sent.

Under the hood

The strategy is a config file. The loop is real code.

Claudecontent generation and performance synthesis
HubSpotcampaign execution: contacts, segments, sends, logging
SQLitethe memory: engagement history that persists across runs
Pythonthe loop itself: orchestration of all four stages
Flaskthe window into it: dashboard and run reports

Three engineering decisions, each with a marketing reason: the pipeline ships with a built-in demo mode (anyone can run the full loop in one command, with the real Claude + HubSpot integrations in the code); engagement is simulated over a full segment audience the way a real email tool reports it; and adding a company is a single-file change, which is what makes it a reusable system, not a one-off.

The takeaway

The automation was the easy part. The leverage came from deciding what should remain judgment.

That is the project in one sentence, and it is how I approach product marketing generally: find the loop, decide which parts deserve a human, and build the rest so the human's judgment compounds.
